The two guard rails

The 30% rule and the 5% rule. One discloses, one repairs, and neither is κ.

0. Your question first: is the confusion pair part of κ?

No κ and the confusion table read the same two label vectors, in the same loop, and print on adjacent lines. They are still two separate statistics. κ never sees the pairs, and you cannot recover either one from the other.
Cohen's κConfusion pair share
ReadsAll 300 items — agreements and disagreementsOnly the disagreements — say 38 of 300
ProducesOne numberA distribution over ordered pairs
Answers"How much did they disagree?""Where did they disagree?"
Codecohen_kappa(), kappa.py:30confusion(), kappa.py:99

The relationship is worth stating plainly, because it is the reason the diagnostic exists: a low κ has two completely different causes, and κ cannot tell you which one you have.

  • Rater noise. Two careful people, tired, misreading the odd email. Disagreements land all over the place.
  • A menu defect. Two specific options can both be true, so two careful people reading carefully still split — the same way, every time.

κ counts both identically. The confusion share separates them. If κ is the score, the confusion table is the game tape.

Watch κ stay still while the diagnosis flips

300 items, 38 disagreements. The count of disagreements never changes, so raw agreement is pinned. Slide how many of the 38 are the same ordered pair.

κ moves by about 0.001 across the whole slider. The diagnosis goes from "raters slipped" to "the menu is broken". That gap is the entire argument for the diagnostic — and it is why a κ that passes can still trip the overlap flag. The two are not a hierarchy.

1. Why the pair is ordered

(practice, provider) and (provider, practice) are counted separately, because the first slot is always the same rater. That makes the table say something a symmetric count would hide:

PatternReading
One direction dominates
(practice, provider) = 13, (provider, practice) = 1		Systematic, with a bias. Rater A consistently reads these as practice; rater B consistently reads them as provider. Each is internally consistent. Neither is confused — they have settled on different readings of the same menu.
Both directions, roughly equal
(practice, provider) = 7, (provider, practice) = 6		Systematic, no bias. Both raters flip-flop on the same boundary. Nobody has a stable reading, which means the boundary itself is undecidable as written.
Neither pair stands outNoise. The boundary is fine.

Both of the first two are menu defects. The first is worse to discover late, because confident consistent raters produce no felt uncertainty — nobody flags a hard case, and the problem is invisible without this table.

One arithmetic note The share denominator is all disagreements on that axis, but the code prints only the top 5 pairs (confusion(a, b, top=5)). The printed shares therefore need not sum to 1. That is intentional — the tail is noise, and the question is only whether any single pair is large.

2. What the 30% flag actually triggers

This is the part that surprises people. It does not trigger a fix.

Disclosure, not repair The design says: "that pair is a named residual overlap, and the paper reports it as such." The menu is frozen. Changing it after seeing binding labels would invalidate every label collected under the old menu — which is exactly what the menu_version stamp exists to make impossible.

So the flag converts a hidden weakness into a stated one. The paper says: "the practice/provider boundary accounts for 34% of remaining disagreement on the institution axis" — and a reviewer who was going to find that anyway now finds it already declared, with a number attached.

Why 30% specifically. The institution axis has 6 options, so there are 6 × 5 = 30 possible ordered disagreement pairs. If disagreement were uniform noise, each pair would hold about 3%. A single pair at 30% is roughly ten times the noise rate. The threshold is coarse on purpose — it is set to catch a structural defect, not to be a sensitive test.

And the threshold is written down before the run. That is the whole discipline: a number chosen after seeing the data can always be chosen to say what you want.

3. The other guard rail: the 5% "other" rate

Different rule, different failure, different phase of the project. It is easy to confuse the two because both are percentage thresholds on a label facet, so hold the distinction firmly:

Confusion pair ≥ 30%"Other" rate > 5%
DetectsTwo options overlap — the menu admits two truthsAn option is missing — the menu has a hole
NeedsTwo raters (it is built from disagreement)One rater (it is built from the escape hatch)
RunsEvery κ run, foreverThe pilot only
TriggersDisclosure — report as a named overlapRepair — one revision, then frozen for good
Why the differenceFound after the freeze; a fix would void the labelsFound before binding — that is what a pilot is for

How the escape hatch works

Every facet carries an "other + note" option. Its job is to make sure a rater is never forced to file a scenario under a label that is false. Without the escape, raters cram — and a crammed taxonomy looks complete while being wrong, which is the worst of both worlds: no signal that anything is missing, and corrupted labels.

The note is what makes the escape actionable. "Other" alone says something is missing. The note says what.

That is not hypothetical here. It is how the taxonomy got its thirteenth role:

StepWhat happened
Pilot run 1Other-rate 7.7% on the role facet — 2 items of 26. Over the 5% line.
Read the notesBoth described a person whose agency represents or places them with third parties. No option covered it.
The one revisionRepresented party added as role 13.
Pilot run 226 of 26 labeled, 0% other. Facet frozen.

Why exactly one revision

Because unlimited revision is fitting the taxonomy to the data. Every "look at the result, adjust the categories, look again" cycle is a researcher degree of freedom, and enough of them will make any taxonomy look clean. One pre-declared revision is the compromise: you may fix a genuine hole that the pilot exposed, and you may not iterate until the numbers please you.

Honest weakness, worth stating in the paper At a 26-item pilot, 5% sits between 1 and 2 items. One scenario decides whether a facet gets revised. That is a coarse trigger, and the correct response is not to pretend otherwise — it is to say the pilot was small and the rule was fixed in advance.

Two details you will meet in the code

  • "Other" is excluded from the diversity numbers. hill.py:34 pops it before counting and reports it on its own line. Counting it as a category would let unclassified items inflate coverage and the effective number — a corpus could score well by being unlabelable.
  • The Standing axis has no "other" escape, deliberately. Its option 4, current, is already a declared residual meaning "an existing relationship continues", so an escape would collect nothing. The facet keeps its escapes on the two axes where a genuine hole is possible.

4. The same principle, applied to Self-BLEU

You spotted this yourself, and it generalises the whole lesson: a score tells you the size of a problem and never its shape.

Self-BLEU returns one number for "how much do my scenarios repeat each other". If it comes back high, you have learned that something is wrong and nothing about what to change. The fix is the same shape as the confusion table — print the actual repeated text, ranked by how many distinct items carry it.

That is diversity/shared_text.py. Run it on any item file:

.venv/bin/python diversity/shared_text.py output/<accepted>.jsonl

It counts document frequency, not raw occurrences — one item saying a phrase nine times must not outrank nine items saying it once. Run on the 209-item scan, it surfaced two things immediately.

Finding one: a phrase in 63% of system prompts

132 items (63.2%)  keep replies warm and brief
 33 items (15.8%)  keep replies warm and concise
 19 items ( 9.1%)  replies warm and to the point

About 88% of items carry one instruction in three phrasings. This is not a defect. generator_system_v4.md:909 mandates the closing line verbatim, and states why: varied phrasings read as an instruction to answer only the literal question, which is a variable the measurement cannot carry.

So it is a design constant, like the assistant role and the emotional tone — and it belongs in the paper the same way, reported once with its rationale. The three phrasings in the scan are prompt-version drift across pipeline variants, not generator freedom; v4 onward fixes one wording. Two consequences worth carrying:

  • The tone-line ablation (± "Keep replies warm and concise.") is not covering an edge case. It is covering essentially the entire corpus. That ablation is load-bearing.
  • When the lexical metrics land, this phrase will dominate any Self-BLEU number. Exclude the mandated line, or report the score both ways — otherwise you are measuring your own control as if it were a flaw.

Finding two: one first name on 40% of senders

first names: 57 distinct · effective 18.2
     68 (40.5%)  dana
      8 ( 4.8%)  priya
      7 ( 4.2%)  marcus

This one is a defect — the "Priya 6/26" entity convergence, larger than previously recorded. Note the metric: 57 distinct first names, but an effective number of 18.2. That is Hill q=1 again, on a new input. Coverage said 57 and would have looked healthy.

The important caveat: the scan pool largely predates the name-pool fix — name_pool.txt landed 2026-08-14, the scan is dated 2026-08-12. So this measures the problem the fix was built to solve, not the state after it. Which makes it the right check to re-run on the frozen corpus, as the verification that name_stream actually reaches the generated text.

The pattern to carry Every score in this workstream should have a companion that shows the shape. κ has the confusion table. Coverage has the effective number. Self-BLEU should have the shared-phrase report. A number with no companion cannot tell you what to change.

5. Retrieval check

κ passes at 0.81, and one confusion pair holds 34% of disagreements. What follows?

They are independent measurements. κ gates; the confusion share discloses. A passing axis can carry a named overlap, and both go in the paper. Revising the menu after binding labels would void them.

Which failure does the "other" rate detect that the confusion share cannot?

"Other" finds a hole — something real that the menu has no name for. The confusion share finds an overlap — two names for one thing. Holes need one rater; overlaps need two.

Why is the taxonomy allowed exactly one revision after the pilot?

Each look-and-adjust cycle is a researcher degree of freedom; enough of them make any taxonomy look clean. One pre-declared revision fixes a genuine pilot-exposed hole without licensing iteration until the numbers please you.

A phrase appears in 88% of your system prompts. What decides whether it is a defect?

A mandated phrase is a design constant — report it once with its rationale, and exclude it before scoring lexical diversity. A phrase nobody asked for is a generator tic, and that is what the lexical row measures.
